Digital Systems II

EEEE-220 RIT, Fall 2025

A RISC processor in VHDL

Course project, October to December 2025

Designed a reduced instruction set processor with Harvard architecture (separate program and data memory) and a two-bus datapath. It has a structurally built ALU, IN and OUT instructions for input and output, shift-left and rotate-through-carry instructions, and conditional jumps. I wrote the control unit and datapath in VHDL in Quartus Prime and verified them in ModelSim.

To prove the whole design worked, the processor ran a multiply program: given 15 from the switches, it worked through about 93 instructions and put 0xE1 (225) on the LEDs. Debugging meant tracing instructions cycle by cycle through waveforms and memory, including fixing a control unit bug that loaded the wrong register.

  • VHDL
  • Quartus Prime
  • ModelSim
  • Processor design
  • FPGA

Labs

  • Labs 1–2Built a counter on the FPGA, then wrote a multiplexer and full adder three different ways in Verilog, each with my own testbench.
  • Lab 4Wrote multiplexers and a ripple-carry adder in both Verilog and VHDL and ran the adder on the FPGA.
  • Labs 5–6Built an ALU in Verilog and VHDL covering arithmetic, logic, shifts, and rotates, and tested it on the FPGA.
  • Lab 7Stored my name in a ROM and displayed it one ASCII character per clock cycle.
  • Labs 8–10Designed the processor's datapath, control unit, and instruction cycles, which became the RISC processor above.

Embedded Systems Design

EEEE-420 RIT, Spring 2026

Touch-driven data display

Final project, March 2026, with an IEEE-format report

Programmed an MSP430 in a mix of C and assembly to read five capacitive touch pads. The user picks a sample rate (100 or 200 Hz) and a scale (linear or base-2 logarithmic) by touching the wheel. The board then samples an analog input 20 times with its 10-bit ADC, shows each sample on a multiplexed ring of eight LEDs, and streams the values over UART at 9600 baud. All timing comes from the chip's hardware timers.

Tested with sine, square, and sawtooth inputs in all four rate and scale combinations. Along the way I fixed a wiring quirk where the upper four LEDs share pins with inverted logic, evened out LED brightness across multiplexing frames, and tracked down a timer that kept leftover settings between its two jobs.

  • MSP430
  • C
  • Assembly
  • Capacitive touch
  • ADC
  • Hardware timers
  • UART

Labs

  • Labs 1–3Set up the MSP430 tools, traced register values step by step while code ran, and learned the core assembly instructions and directives.
  • Lab 4Filled and summed arrays in memory, then wrote a multiply routine in assembly and again in C.
  • Lab 5Drove the booster pack's ring of eight LEDs in spinning patterns, working around the reversed wiring on half of them.
  • Lab 6Dimmed and brightened an LED with pulse-width modulation by switching between 80% and 20% duty cycles, in assembly and C.
  • Lab 7Measured each touch pad untouched and touched, then set thresholds to tell a real touch from noise.
  • Lab 9Read the chip's built-in temperature sensor through the ADC and got about 19.6 °C, close to the room's actual temperature.
  • Lab 11Combined touch input, ADC sampling, and the LED display, which grew into the final project above.

Digital Systems I

EEEE-120 RIT, Spring 2025

Bit-serial adder/subtractor

Final project, April 2025

Designed a 5-bit adder/subtractor that works one bit at a time. Two shift registers feed a serial adder, an accumulator collects the result, and a single finite state machine controls loading, adding or subtracting, shifting, and signaling when it's done. Built in Quartus Prime and verified with gate-level simulation in ModelSim.

  • ModelSim
  • FSM design
  • Shift registers

Digital Electronics

EEEE-380 RIT, Spring 2026

Designing logic at the transistor level: sizing transistors by hand, then checking every design in LTspice.

  • Lab 1Learned LTspice by simulating an NMOS transistor and working backward from its current to find its channel length.
  • Lab 2Analyzed NMOS inverters with resistor and transistor loads. My hand-calculated output high of 1.15 V matched the 1.12 V simulation within 2.6%.
  • Lab 3Sized a CMOS inverter so its two noise margins came out equal (1.47 V and 1.46 V in simulation), measured its rise and fall times, and built a five-stage ring oscillator.
  • Lab 4Built CMOS logic gates, including a three-input NAND, and checked its truth table and noise margins.
  • Lab 5Designed a CMOS D latch to meet a timing spec and analyzed a one-shot (monostable) circuit by hand and in simulation.
  • Lab 6Showed how charge sharing breaks a domino logic gate: I predicted the node would drop to 1.414 V, the simulation showed 1.41 V, and adding a small keeper transistor fixed the wrong output.
  • Lab 7Designed a six-transistor SRAM memory cell and simulated writing and reading a stored 1 without disturbing it.

Electric Vehicle Team

Student team, RIT October 2024 to September 2025

Audio driver

Team of six, my part: the microphone board

An audio system for an electric vehicle that can record and play back sound. A main board with an STM32 microcontroller and SD card connects to separate speaker and microphone boards over the Automotive Audio Bus, which carries both power and data on a single twisted pair.

Each team member owned a different part of the system, and mine was the microphone board, the part that records audio. I worked on its circuit in Altium Designer: two MEMS microphones capture left and right channels and send the audio over I2S to the board's bus chip, which passes it back to the STM32. I also researched vehicle-compatible components and helped combine several boards into a single PCB to save space.

  • Altium Designer
  • STM32
  • PCB design
  • I2S
  • Automotive Audio Bus

Optics and analog signals

Personal build

Laser vibrometry microphone

Finished, based on a design guide by @1nska

An optical microphone that picks up sound without being near it. Sound makes a window pane vibrate by a few nanometers, and an infrared laser bounced off the glass carries those tiny vibrations back to a receiver.

The transmitter is an 808 nm laser diode driven by an LM317 set up as a current regulator, which holds the laser at a steady current of about 266 mA. The receiver starts with a BPW34 photodiode feeding one TL074 quad op-amp chip. The first stage is a transimpedance amplifier that turns the faint photocurrent into a voltage (1 V per microamp), the second doubles that signal, and the third is a two-step low-pass filter with a buffer that strips out high-frequency noise. The last stage is an LM567 tone decoder, whose output goes low when it detects the signal.

The op-amps run from a ±9 V split supply made from two 9 V batteries, and a 7805 regulator turns that into 5 V for the laser driver and the tone decoder.

  • LM317 current regulator
  • BPW34 photodiode
  • TL074 op-amps
  • Transimpedance amplifier
  • Active filters
  • LM567 tone decoder
  • Split power supply
